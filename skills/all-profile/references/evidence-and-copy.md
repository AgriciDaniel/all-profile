# Evidence and copy decisions

## Claims that can travel across profiles

For each consequential claim retain the source URL or supplied artifact, capture date, exact observation, allowed wording, and limitation. Mark owner-provided claims as such; they are not independent verification. When sources disagree, retain the conflict and use the better-supported, narrower wording or omit the number.

For numerical proof, name the metric and exact item set, avoid duplicate items, and recompute totals with code. A rounded display is not an exact integer. Refresh volatile proof before a new publication; if inaccessible, use accurate qualitative wording and report the gap. Never imply all members take part in an activity just because the community offers it.

Good proof can be a portfolio, published project, credential, attributed recognition, documented process, or measured result with its context. High follower counts are not required. A role or first-person process must be true for the subject. Testimonials need attributable evidence and permission for the intended use, not a generated paraphrase presented as a quote.

## Learn from references without borrowing a persona

Inspect a supplied screenshot before drawing conclusions about its layout. Identify what each segment does: association, work, scale, recognition, invitation. Transfer useful hierarchy and rhythm, not another person's awards, credentials, personal symbols, or superlatives.

A proof-rich headline can still be clear. Do not automatically remove named projects, numbers, or emojis in the name of restraint. Choose according to the brief, audience comprehension, evidence, and available space. Pair icons with meaningful words and check screen-reader sense and narrow wrapping.

When using a video, distinguish caption coverage from actual visual coverage. Record inspected intervals and timestamp the relevant advice. Automatic captions support a paraphrase with uncertainty; they do not prove unseen visual details. Do not describe a partial visual check as full audiovisual analysis. Do not require a paid video-analysis service to adapt a useful idea.

## Writing pattern

Long field: association, truthful work/process, a few specific examples, scoped proof, then the next step. Short field: recognizable association, strongest compact proof, then invitation if the destination is available. These are useful defaults, not a mandatory template for every person.

Separate paste-ready profile text from review notes. Keep necessary claim scope in the profile, such as “stars across three projects,” but put arithmetic, export provenance, verification caveats, and tool limitations in the accompanying evidence notes. Do not make a visitor read the agent's verification process as part of the person's biography. Include dates or citations in public copy only when they help the reader or the user requests them.

Fictional illustration, not evidence about a real person:

> I build accessible learning websites for small education teams. Explore my three public interface demos and their accessibility notes. See the portfolio for examples.

This demonstrates concrete work and inspectable evidence without inventing customers or impact. If the demos are only prototypes, say so. If no proof is supplied, omit the proof clause and explain what would strengthen it.

Keep the destination promise precise. If the approved free resource cannot be linked yet and the current link is paid, show that mismatch in the proposal. Offer to defer the CTA or use accurate neutral wording for approval. Removing the word “free” does not by itself resolve a confusing destination.

Do not imply a link is clickable when only plain text is available. Directional arrows need an actual visible destination in that direction. Avoid repeating the CTA in every field unless the user wants that treatment.

## Search and trust claims

Consistent profiles can be evaluated for clarity and factual agreement. Do not promise AI recommendations, ranking gains, or conversion lift without relevant evidence. E-E-A-T may inform editorial questions about authorship, work, and trust; it is not a social-profile certification or a numerical platform score. Verify current Google wording before attributing claims to it.
